Section 01Target user

Not a demographic.
A household with two pincodes.

28–38
BLR · HYD · PUNE
The Remote Provider
Working professional Lives away from parents Already pays family's bills Owns a Gaana or Spotify plan

An urban professional in their late 20s or 30s who has moved cities for work and already pays recurring bills for the family back home — a parent's mobile recharge, an insurance premium, a shared OTT login. They want to add their parents and siblings to one music plan, but every family plan on the market is built for people who live under one roof, not people who share one bill across two cities.

02
Section 02Problem

Family plans assume
a family shares a roof.

It doesn't for this listener — and the current alternatives are built around that wrong assumption in two very different, equally unhelpful ways.

The friction today

Spotify's Family plan requires every member to verify they live at the exact same residential address, re-checked periodically. By Spotify's own stated policy, a member who misses the 7-day window can rejoin the same plan the moment the manager sends a fresh invite — the 12-month lockout only applies to joining a different Family or Duo plan.

In practice, that distinction gets lost. Spotify's own community forums carry dozens of threads where the same-plan rejoin fails anyway — a reported glitch that leaves long-time members stuck on the free tier until they raise a support ticket and wait for a manual override, just to get back onto a plan they'd been on for years.

For this listener, "family" is a parent in one city and a sibling in another. There is no shared address to verify, so the plan simply cannot be built the way it wants to be built.

Why YouTube doesn't help either

YouTube Premium does offer household sharing — up to 6 people on a Family plan and 2 people on its two-person plan in India. But both require members to live in the same household, with YouTube performing a 30-day electronic check-in.

That leaves a clear gap for the Remote Provider: they may be willing to pay for family members who live elsewhere, but YouTube's shared plans aren't designed for that use case. The alternative is for those family members to maintain individual subscriptions or remain on the free, ad-supported experience.

Why a bigger player can't copy this fast

This isn't a Spotify quirk — Spotify and YouTube both enforce the same address-based rule, and both have actively tightened it since late 2025. It's a converging industry policy, not a switch either company can quietly flip off for India.

Loosening it means unwinding global anti-fraud infrastructure built to protect margins everywhere they operate — a slow, high-scrutiny call for either company. Gaana is a single-market operator that can design the rule correctly from day one, and its real cost driver (per-stream royalties, not per-title licensing) gives it a cleaner lever to control cost without an address check at all.

The chosen branch, in detail
Gaana Ghar Plan
"Ghar" — your people, not your pincode

The plan owner adds up to 4 family members by name, relationship, and mobile number — no address field, anywhere. Each member gets their own login, their own language and taste profile, and their own recommendations. Instead of policing where people live, the plan controls Gaana's real cost — streaming royalties — by capping concurrent streams (up to 3 devices playing at once), which is the lever that actually protects margin. The owner manages the whole roster from one dashboard and can swap a member out instantly — no 12-month lockouts, ever.

Impact

High

Converts a bill the Remote Provider already pays elsewhere into Gaana's highest-revenue plan per owner, and pulls in family members who'd otherwise never pay for music at all — hitting "start paying," "pay more," and "stay longer" at once.

Effort

Medium

No geo-verification system to build — the hardest, most error-prone part of Spotify's own model. Reuses existing accounts and profiles; the real net-new work is the invite flow and a simple concurrent-stream cap.

Confidence

Medium-high

The mechanic is proven elsewhere; the pain it removes is loudly, currently documented on Spotify's own support forums. Both halves of the bet are already evidenced.

04
Section 04Why this will work

One company already
ran this exact experiment.

Comparable product — Netflix Extra Member
9.33M

Netflix's household crackdown pushed people who shared an account outside their home to either buy their own plan or pay a lower add-on price as an "Extra Member" — its own login, its own profile, no shared address required. Netflix reported the move was revenue-positive in every region, with low cancellation, and added 9.33 million paid subscribers in a single quarter after rolling it out globally.

Ghar Plan borrows the pricing lever, not the whole model: Netflix's core household still gets address-verified, and the flat add-on works because its cost per title barely changes with viewers. Gaana pays labels per stream, so Ghar Plan drops the address check entirely and pairs the per-head fee with a concurrent-stream cap to protect the cost Netflix doesn't have to manage.

The pain, in the competitor's own forum
Stuck

Spotify's own policy says a member who misses verification can rejoin the same plan immediately once re-invited — the 12-month lock only applies to switching to a different plan. But its community support forum carries dozens of threads from multi-year Family plan members whose same-plan rejoin failed anyway, a reported glitch that leaves them stuck on the free tier until Spotify support manually intervenes. Several say they're ready to cancel entirely rather than fight it — exactly the friction a no-address model removes by design.
